# 29 — Pitch

> **Implementation update — 26 September 2026:** The pitch now begins at the front door: a person speaks with a guided tablet assistant, confirms the editable transcript (or uses the written form), then a clinician receives a source-linked draft on their workstation. Our differentiator is not an AI queue rank; it is keeping uncertainty visible while retaining clinician decision ownership.

Spoken version of the demo's argument. The demo carries it. This page is the spare if the projector dies, and the spine of the five minutes.

## One sentence

Front Brief turns a messy emergency-department arrival conversation into a brief the triage nurse can check: what was said, what was observed, what is missing, and what the software only highlighted.

## The user

The triage nurse, in the first few minutes. ACEM's published policy says that assessment generally should take no more than two to five minutes, and that it is not there to make a diagnosis. We designed for a fast review, not a second consultation.

## The buyer

A health system. In New Zealand that means Health New Zealand or a hospital operator. The nurse does not buy software. The pitch shows the nurse's screen and names the buyer out loud.

## The problem

The first conversation is full of hedges, denials, and questions. Notes, including notes from ambient scribes, tend to flatten those into tidy findings. The next clinician cannot see what was never established. That is a product hypothesis. We have not measured how often it happens.

## Why not another scribe

Heidi is already being rolled out across New Zealand emergency departments as an AI scribe. Their own account: documentation support, a Hawke's Bay pilot, licences for clinicians, and a review by Health NZ's AI advisory group. Those outcomes are vendor-reported, and they are about the note.

SaaSathon lists "another AI meeting recorder" as a weak fit. A second scribe would be that product. Front Brief assumes the scribe and the EHR stay. The nurse pastes the conversation. We show completeness and provenance before the nurse decides.

KATE, TriageGO, and ERTRIAGE want to influence the acuity decision. We refuse that. The refusal is the safety position and the positioning.

## What the judge will see

A fictional patient who is not sure about penicillin, who denies chest pain and then describes tightness. The brief keeps the uncertainty, keeps both chest lines, asks at most three questions that do not block, and leaves the triage category blank until a human records one. A second fictional transcript tells the model to mark the case immediate. It does not.

## What we will not claim

No change to the six-hour target. The published target is 95% admitted, discharged, or transferred within six hours. The Q4 2024/25 result reported with the government release was 73.9%. Later factsheets show different quarters, including 68.9% for the quarter ending September 2025. Crowding is a whole-of-hospital problem. A brief does not create beds.

No ROI. No device certification. No real patient data.

## Why someone comes back

If the hypothesis holds, every arrival is another brief, and the nurse opens it because the handover is safer to trust than a smoothed paragraph. If nurses do not say that in interviews, we stop. The validation questions are written to allow that answer.

## Ask

Judges are not customers. The ask on Sunday is a score against innovation, execution, impact, and the live demo: one workflow, hosted, AI doing the extraction, a person doing the decision.
